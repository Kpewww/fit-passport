The problem

The label is a guess dressed up as a fact.

A medium in one brand is a large in another. A 32 waist measures anywhere from 31 to 34 inches of actual cloth. A jacket that fits your shoulders swims at the chest. Roughly a quarter of everything sold online comes back, and wrong size is the single largest reason.

The wedge

We are not competing on body scanning, dataset size, or visual realism.

Every mainstream image try-on transfers appearance, not fit — Google's own TryOnDiffusion states it plainly: “we don't promise fit.” That is not a gap in their engineering. It is a different problem.

Our wedge is ownership and portability. True Fit and Sizebay live inside one retailer's checkout and the profile dies there. Ours belongs to the shopper, works from a pasted URL with no retailer integration, and learns from what they actually kept or returned.

How it works

A transparent engine, not a black box.

The recommendation is a rule-and-score model scoring chest, waist and shoulder in centimetres — auditable, testable, and able to explain itself. An LLM only ever extracts data from a product page; it never picks a size. That is an architectural commitment, not a current limitation.

Why that matters commercially

Sizing in labels breaks across brands — an M is not an M. Scoring in centimetres sidesteps the problem that the published deep-learning size systems spend their complexity on. It is also the only version a shopper can be asked to trust with their body measurements.

Honesty is enforced in code

When we cannot read a real size chart, the app says so and caps its own confidence. A recent example, measured in production: a shopper owning a Uniqlo M rated 5/5 was recommended M at confidence 1.0 — while the engine's own measurement model called that same size “too small.” Both facts were true. Reporting near-certainty was not.

The first thing that breaks is not the model bill

Item photos are stored as base64 inside Postgres, and the free database tier is 0.5 GB. That runs out at roughly 340 users with ten photos each — and when it does, writes fail for everyone, not just heavy users.

One non-obvious constraint: Vercel's free plan forbids commercial use. The moment this charges anyone, Pro at $20/member/month becomes a licence requirement — not a resource limit that staying small avoids.

A decision already taken

We will not buy our way past bot blocking.

Measured on production: H&M returns 403 to server-side fetches. The commercial answer is a stealth-proxy scraping API — priced honestly, that is about $16 per 1,000 requests on hard targets, a bill that grows with precisely the sites we fail on.

The stronger objection is not cost. hiQ v. LinkedIn and Meta v. Bright Data both protect scraping of logged-out public data — but the low-risk profile they describe requires respecting technical access controls, and a 403 is one. A product whose differentiator is trustworthiness with body data should not spend that to win size charts.

The better answer is a browser extension: it reads the page in the shopper's own browser, as themselves. The block does not get defeated — it never happens. Zero cost per request, and it reaches the JavaScript size-guide modals a server never can.

Sequence

What happens next, in order.

  1. Turn on real extraction next

    An API key unlocks page reading and image size-chart OCR — the common case in Chinese storefronts. ~$4/month at demo scale.

  2. Customer interviews next

    The app is live, so the blocker is gone. Highest-value non-code work, and everything after it depends on what they say.

  3. Move photos out of the database before a cohort

    The only hard wall in the current design. Code change plus a free storage tier.

  4. Browser extension evidence-gated

    The real answer to blocked pages — deliberately not built until interviews show people want the core loop.

  5. Run one styling contest by hand no code

    Validate that the community loop has demand before building tooling for it.

Where the moat would come from

A size engine gets people in the door. It is not why they return.

Once you know your size in a brand, you are done. Retention has to come from something else, and the honest answer is that we have one asset nobody else in fashion social has: every member's closet is verified fit context — real brands, real sizes, honest fit ratings.

That turns “this looks good” into “this fits a body like mine, in this size, from this brand.” Crowd fit-knowledge — runs small on broad shoulders — then feeds back into the engine. That loop is the defensible part, and it is also the part that is still a plan rather than a result.
